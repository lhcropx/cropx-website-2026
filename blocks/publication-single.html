Case Study Alfalfa

40% Water Reduction on a 125-Acre Arizona Alfalfa Pivot

March 2026 6 min read Arizona, USA
Featured Image Maricopa County, Arizona — August 2025

Key findings

40% Reduction in water consumption across the trial period
125 acres Alfalfa pivot fully automated with CropX sensors
0 Manual interventions required during the trial period
Share

Center-pivot irrigation is the backbone of large-scale alfalfa production in the arid Southwest — but in Arizona's Maricopa County, water allocations are tightening and energy costs for pumping from depth have risen sharply. This case study documents how one 125-acre alfalfa operation deployed CropX soil sensors across a single pivot, automated its irrigation scheduling entirely, and achieved a 40% reduction in applied water across two consecutive cuttings without any measurable loss in dry-matter yield.

The Challenge

The grower, a third-generation farming operation near Buckeye, Arizona, had irrigated this pivot using a fixed calendar schedule inherited from years of practice: three-day cycle times regardless of temperature, humidity, or stage of crop growth. The approach had worked well enough in a wetter decade, but with groundwater costs rising and state-level conservation incentives creating pressure to demonstrate efficiency gains, continuing on autopilot was no longer viable.

Alfalfa is a demanding crop in desert conditions. Summer temperatures routinely exceed 110°F, driving evapotranspiration (ET) rates of 0.45–0.55 inches per day during peak heat. Any deficit in root-zone moisture during peak ET translates almost immediately into wilting and, if sustained, quality loss in the cut. At the same time, over-irrigation — particularly in the dense, clay-loam soils common to this region — causes nitrogen leaching, waterlogging of the crown, and Phytophthora root rot. The agronomic target is a narrow band, and hitting it consistently with a static schedule is largely a matter of luck.

The CropX Solution

In spring 2025, CropX deployed a four-sensor array across the 125-acre pivot, positioning probes at representative soil zones identified through an initial EC scan of the field. Sensors were installed at 20 cm and 50 cm depths at each location, providing both shallow root-zone and deeper profile moisture data. The center-pivot's control panel was connected directly to the CropX platform via its existing cellular uplink, enabling automated pivot start/stop commands based on model outputs without any hardware modifications to the irrigation system itself.

The CropX agronomic model integrated soil moisture readings with local weather station data and a 7-day ET forecast to generate a dynamic irrigation schedule updated every 15 minutes. Rather than running the pivot on a fixed cycle, the system triggered irrigation only when volumetric water content at the 20 cm depth crossed the crop-specific depletion threshold — set at 45% of available water capacity for alfalfa in loam soil. This threshold-based approach meant that on cooler overcast days, the pivot might sit idle for 96 hours; during peak July heat, it could run two shorter cycles within a 36-hour window. The scheduling logic in both cases was handled autonomously, with no operator intervention required.

"We used to set the timer on Sunday night and hope for the best. Now the system knows more about what's happening in that field than I could ever figure out by walking it — and it's making better calls than I was."

— Grower, Buckeye, Arizona

Results

Across the 2025 first and second cutting period — April through August — the CropX-managed pivot applied 24.6 acre-inches of water, compared to the grower's historical baseline of 41.0 acre-inches over the same seasonal window. This represents a 40% reduction in applied water. Dry-matter yield measured at 5.2 tons per acre for the two combined cuttings, consistent with the three-year average from this field under the prior calendar-based regime. No yield penalty was observed, and stem quality metrics — critical for dairy-grade alfalfa markets — held within acceptable tolerance bands.

Energy savings from reduced pump runtime translated to an estimated $4,200 in reduced electricity costs for the period, against an annualized CropX subscription cost of $1,800 for the sensor array and connectivity. The payback period for the initial hardware investment was projected at under eight months. Beyond the direct cost savings, the operation was awarded a water conservation credit under Arizona's Groundwater Management Act, representing an additional $2,900 in regulatory offset value for the season.

Methodology

CropX sensors were calibrated using soil samples collected at installation, with laboratory texture analysis confirming loam classification and field capacity at 32% VWC. The agronomic model applied an alfalfa crop coefficient (Kc) curve derived from published ASCE reference ET data and adjusted for the Arizona desert climate zone. Baseline comparison figures reflect the grower's metered pump records from the preceding three seasons on the same pivot. All yield measurements were conducted by a third-party agronomist at harvest using calibrated scale equipment. No supplemental treatments or fertility program changes were made during the trial period to isolate the irrigation effect.

This case study demonstrates that sensor-driven, threshold-based automation is not merely incremental improvement over calendar scheduling — it is a fundamentally different management paradigm. When the system has accurate, real-time data on what is actually happening in the root zone, and the authority to act on it autonomously, the result is irrigation that matches actual crop need rather than one that approximates it. In a water-constrained environment like Arizona, closing that gap from approximation to precision has both agronomic and economic consequences that compound across every cutting, every season.
