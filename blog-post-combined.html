Why Soil Moisture Data Is the Most Underused Asset on Your Farm

Farmers collect more data than ever — but most of it sits unused. Here's how continuous soil moisture monitoring transforms raw readings into decisions that actually improve yield and water efficiency.

June 18, 2026 Dr. Sarah Okonkwo 7 min read

The average commercial farm in the United States generates over 500 gigabytes of agronomic data per year. Soil pH readings, weather station outputs, satellite imagery, yield maps — the data is there. But according to a 2025 survey by the American Farm Bureau, fewer than 12% of growers report using that data to make in-season irrigation decisions.

The gap isn't a data problem. It's a translation problem. Most farms don't lack sensors — they lack a system that turns sensor output into a clear next action.

Why Soil Moisture Is Different

Not all farm data ages at the same rate. A yield map from 2023 is still informative. A weather forecast from two days ago is useless. Soil moisture sits in the middle — it's a living signal that changes within hours during irrigation or a rain event, but its patterns reveal something durable: how your specific soil profile, at a specific location on your field, moves and holds water.

That pattern — what hydrologists call the field water capacity curve — is uniquely yours. Two fields separated by a fence row can behave completely differently during an irrigation event. Understanding those differences is the foundation of any precision water management strategy.

The Data Translation Gap

Here's where most farm data platforms fall short. They present raw readings — a graph of volumetric water content over time — and leave the interpretation to the grower. That's like handing someone a blood pressure cuff and expecting them to diagnose hypertension. The number alone isn't enough; you need context.

"We had sensors in the ground for two years before we understood what they were actually telling us. The data was there — we just didn't know which numbers to act on."

— Marcus Elstad, row-crop farmer, Minnesota

Effective soil moisture interpretation requires layering the raw signal against at least three additional variables: rooting depth at the current crop growth stage, the evapotranspiration rate for the past 48 hours, and the forecast for the next 5–7 days.

From Data to Decision

Setting thresholds that match your soil

The single most impactful thing a grower can do is calibrate irrigation thresholds to their specific soil texture rather than using a generic table. A silt loam and a sandy loam can show identical volumetric water content readings — but one may be at field capacity while the other is well into stress territory.

Key Takeaway

CropX's soil modeling algorithm automatically fits your field's water curve to your soil class, so the threshold you see on screen is already calibrated to your specific conditions — not a regional average.

Timing the refill point

The "refill point" — the moisture level at which irrigation should begin — is where most water waste occurs. Over-irrigating before hitting the refill point wastes water and pushes nitrates below the root zone. Under-irrigating past it causes measurable yield drag, even when the crop shows no visible stress symptoms.

"Fields monitored with continuous sensors consistently used 20–30% less water while maintaining equivalent or higher yields compared to calendar-scheduled irrigation."

Making It Actionable at Scale

For operations managing more than a few hundred acres, the real challenge is aggregation. How do you turn moisture data from 40 different monitoring points into a daily irrigation schedule that a single operator can actually execute?

  • Zone-level summaries that roll up sensor readings into an irrigation management zone, not individual sensor outputs
  • Push notifications triggered only when a zone approaches its refill threshold — reducing noise and alert fatigue
  • Integration with variable-rate irrigation controllers to close the loop between recommendation and action automatically

When those three elements are in place, soil moisture data stops being something you look at and becomes something that works for you — running in the background, flagging what matters, and connecting directly to the equipment doing the irrigating.
